What counts as a qualified sales call when you outsource lead generation

Write the definition, the not-billed list and the dispute rule into the contract before any work starts. Then a no-show or a wrong-fit company costs the agency, not you.

By Myroslav Sabelnykov, Vurell. 25 November 2026

A qualified sales call is a first conversation, actually held, with a company that fits criteria you wrote down before the work started, with a person in the agreed role, and with notes on what was learned: the need, the timing and who decides. Anything short of that should not be on the invoice. The party that carries the cost of a call that does not happen should be the agency, and the contract should say so in plain words.

A common model for outsourced lead generation is to bill per booked meeting. A booked meeting is a calendar entry, not a conversation. Between the entry and a useful call sit no-shows, companies you already talk to, people who cannot decide, and companies outside your market. If the contract does not mention them, you carry all of them.

This guide covers how to write the definition, what to exclude, how to handle disputes, and why it matters who runs the first call. It ends with a checklist you can paste into a contract, and a short section on how we do it at Vurell.

What can go wrong when you pay per booked meeting?

Four things happen often enough to plan for.

The call does not happen. The prospect accepts an invitation and does not appear, or the meeting is booked by an assistant who never told the buyer. If billing starts at the booking, you have paid for nothing and your salesperson has lost an hour.

The company is already yours. It is in your CRM, it is a client, or it is a deal your own team has been working for months. The agency is paid for "finding" a company you already know.

The company does not fit. Too small to afford your product, in a country you do not serve, or a competitor. The meeting is real, but it cannot become a sale.

The person cannot decide. A junior contact agrees to a call to be polite. Your senior salesperson spends 30 minutes learning that the real decision sits with someone who has not heard of you.

None of these are fraud. They follow from paying for an easy-to-count event. The fix is to change what is counted and to write that down before the first message goes out.

How do I write the definition before the work starts?

Put it in the contract as criteria that someone who was not on the call can check afterwards. Four parts cover most cases.

Part What to write Example wording
Company Size range, sector or product type, and the countries you serve "20 to 200 employees, software or IT services, based in the UK or Ireland"
Person The role or seniority of the person on the call "Founder, managing director, head of sales or head of engineering"
What was learned The minimum facts the notes must contain "The need, the timing, who decides and who else is involved"
Held The call took place with the person, on video or by phone "Both sides joined and spoke for the agreed first call"

Two details save arguments later.

First, write the role as a list of titles or a seniority level, not as "a decision-maker". "Decision-maker" is a judgment made after the fact, and the two sides will judge differently. A list of titles can be checked by reading a LinkedIn page.

Second, require notes. A call without notes is hard to dispute and hard to follow up. Three facts are enough to start: what problem they described, when they want it solved, and who signs. If the agency cannot fill those three lines, the call was a conversation, not a qualification.

What should never be billed?

Agree the exclusion list before the pilot starts, in writing. Seven items belong on it:

  1. A company that is already in your CRM or pipeline. Send the agency your list before work begins, and refresh it when you open new deals.
  2. A current client.
  3. A competitor.
  4. A duplicate, meaning the same company reached twice, or a second person at a company already counted.
  5. A company outside the criteria you agreed.
  6. A person who is not in the agreed role.
  7. Any call that did not happen.

The first item is the one buyers forget. It only works if you hand over the list. An agency cannot avoid companies it has never been told about, so sending it is your side of the bargain.

How should disputes work?

You will disagree about some calls. A fixed process is better than a negotiation each time, and it needs three parts: a window to raise the issue, a window to answer, and a rule for what happens to the invoice in the meantime.

A workable version: the buyer flags a call within 5 working days of the handover and says why. The agency checks it against the call notes and the agreed criteria and answers within 5 working days. A disputed call is not invoiced until it is settled.

The last sentence matters most. If a disputed call stays on the invoice and you have to claim a credit later, the agency has no reason to hurry. When the amount is not collected until the question is answered, both sides want it answered fast.

Decide one more case in advance: a call that moves. Prospects reschedule. A sensible rule is that a call that moves once still counts when it happens, and one that never happens costs nothing. Without that line, either side can argue that a rescheduled call was a failed one, or that a cancelled one still counts.

Why does it matter who runs the first call?

When the agency books the meeting and your salesperson takes the call, the first time anyone checks the fit is on your time. If the company is wrong, you find out 20 minutes in.

When the agency runs the first call itself, the check happens before the handover. The agency holds the conversation, compares the company and the person against your written criteria, writes the notes, and only then passes the call to your team. A call that fails the criteria never reaches your calendar and never reaches the invoice.

This also changes the incentive. An agency that is paid per booking gains by getting any yes. An agency that is paid only for held calls that pass your criteria gains by finding companies that really fit. You can check this in an offer by asking one question: who is on the first call, and who writes the notes?

There is a cost: a first call run by the agency takes the agency's time. Compare the price per qualified call, not per booking.

What can I paste into an agency contract?

Adapt the wording to your market. The structure is what matters.

Checklist for any lead-generation or call-booking contract:

  • A qualified call is a first call that was held, with a company that matches the written criteria (size, country, sector) and a person in the agreed role (list of titles).
  • The handover includes notes: the need, the timing and who decides.
  • Not billed: companies already in the buyer's CRM or pipeline (buyer supplies the list before start), current clients, competitors, duplicates, companies outside the criteria, people outside the agreed role, and any call that did not happen.
  • Billing starts only after the call is held and handed over, not at booking.
  • The buyer may flag a call within 5 working days of the handover, with a reason. The agency answers within 5 working days. A disputed call is not invoiced until it is settled.
  • A call that moves once still counts when it happens. A call that does not happen is not billed.
  • Who holds the first call is named in the contract, and the criteria are checked on that call before the handover.
  • The buyer can end the arrangement at any time without a minimum term, or the term is stated and the exit conditions are listed.
  • The agency reports each month: messages sent, replies, first calls held, qualified calls handed over.

If an agency will not agree to the third, fifth and seventh points, ask why. The answer tells you how the pricing really works.
