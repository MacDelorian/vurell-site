Pay per meeting, retainer or hybrid: how to pay for outsourced lead generation

A retainer pays for effort, a per-meeting fee pays for events, a hybrid splits the risk. Which one is right depends on how well you can define a good call and how fast you need volume.

By Myroslav Sabelnykov, Vurell. 27 November 2026

There are three common ways to pay an agency for outbound lead generation: a monthly retainer, a fee per meeting, or a mix of a smaller monthly fee and a fee per call. None of them is the best in general. Each one decides who pays when a month goes badly, and that is the question to answer before you compare prices.

The short version: a retainer gives you capacity and learning but no promise of an outcome. A pure per-meeting fee moves the risk to the agency but tends to reward volume over fit. A hybrid shares the risk and usually lines up the incentives best, as long as the thing being counted is defined strictly. This guide shows the three on the same numbers so you can see the difference, then says when each one fits.

What does each model actually pay for?

Model You pay for The agency is paid when Main risk for you Main risk for the agency
Retainer Time, capacity, learning Every month, whatever happens A slow month still costs the full fee A client who leaves early
Per meeting An event A meeting is booked or held Meetings that are easy to book but poor fits Months with no events, unpaid work
Hybrid A smaller base plus an event The base every month, the fee when an event happens A smaller version of both A smaller version of both

The word "event" hides most of the problem. A booked meeting is a calendar entry. A held first call with a company that fits criteria you wrote down is something else. If the contract counts the first, the per-meeting part of any model will drift toward the cheapest thing to produce. The companion guide on what counts as a qualified sales call covers how to write the definition and the list of things that are never billed.

What does a month cost under each model?

The numbers below are assumptions to show the shape, not market prices. They are chosen so that the three models cost the same in a good month. Change them for your own offers.

  • Retainer: €2,000 a month.
  • Per meeting: €400 per held call, no base.
  • Hybrid: €500 a month plus €300 per held call.
Held calls in the month Retainer Per meeting Hybrid Hybrid cost per call
0 €2,000 €0 €500 no call
2 €2,000 (€1,000 per call) €800 €1,100 €550
5 €2,000 (€400 per call) €2,000 €2,000 €400
8 €2,000 (€250 per call) €3,200 €2,900 €363

Read the table by rows. In a month with no calls, the retainer costs you everything and the per-meeting model costs you nothing, so the agency carries the whole loss. In a month with eight calls the retainer is the cheapest by far and the agency has earned less than its work was worth. The hybrid sits between the two in every row: you always pay something, the agency always earns something, and both gain when the calls come.

That is why the per-meeting model is harder to sustain than it looks. The agency must price every call high enough to cover the months with none, and a buyer who sees only the good months thinks the price is steep.

What goes wrong with each one?

Retainer. You buy effort, and effort is hard to inspect. The agency can be busy and still produce nothing you can use. Ask what you receive each month beyond the calls: the list of companies, the messages that were sent, the replies, and who owns them if you stop. If the answer is "a report", be careful.

Per meeting. The agency is paid for an event and can see which events are cheap. If you count booked meetings, you will get meetings with the people who say yes easily. Realised meetings are safer for the buyer: pay when the call is held, and agree in writing how a no-show is handled. A call that moves once and then happens still counts. A call that never happens costs nothing.

Hybrid. The base can be set too high. If the monthly part covers most of the agency's cost and profit, the per-call fee stops mattering and you have a retainer with a small bonus. A useful check is to ask what the base pays for. A low base that covers the sending setup, the list and the weekly report is a fee for work. A high base that covers the agency's margin is a retainer under another name.

Which model fits which buyer?

  • You can define a good call precisely and want to pay only for outcomes. Per meeting, with a strict definition and a held-call rule. Expect a higher price per call, because the agency carries the empty months.
  • You do not yet know who your best buyers are. A retainer, for a short and fixed term, with the learning as the deliverable: which signal, which role, which message. Do not sign a long term for this.
  • You want volume soon and also want proof that the calls are real. A hybrid with a low base and a strict definition of the call.
  • You have a deal size under about €5,000. Be careful with any model that has a large fixed part. The arithmetic of pilot cost against deal size shows how many deals a month's fee must buy back.

Two questions work for every model. Who is on the first call? Who writes the notes? If the answer to both is "your team", the agency's job ends at the booking, and the check on fit happens on your time.

What does the contract need, whichever model you choose?

  • The unit that is counted, defined as a held first call with a company and a person that match written criteria.
  • What is never billed: companies already in your pipeline, clients, competitors, duplicates, people outside the agreed role, and calls that did not happen.
  • A dispute window and a rule that a disputed call is not invoiced until settled.
  • What you keep if you stop: the company list, the messages, every reply.
  • How you stop: any month, or a term that is written down with its exit conditions.

How do we price it at Vurell?

We use a hybrid with a low base. The pilot is €100 a month for the first two months, then €300 a month, plus €300 for each qualified call.
