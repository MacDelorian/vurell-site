How many developer roles in Germany stay open for months?

A monthly signal note for companies that sell development capacity or software to German employers. Snapshot of 16 November 2026, compared with 16 October.

By Myroslav Sabelnykov, Vurell. 25 November 2026

This note is for companies that sell to German employers: dev studios, outsourcers, SaaS and custom-software firms. It is not for people looking for a developer job.

On 16 November 2026, 1,561 developer postings from small and mid-sized German employers were on the Federal Employment Agency's job board. 1,066 of them (68%) had been listed for 30 days or more, and 674 (43%) for 90 days or more. The median posting had been listed for 68 days. Each one is a company with a dated, public, unfilled need for the skills you sell.

The numbers below come from our own dataset, read on 16 November 2026 and 16 October 2026. The full data is at German developer hiring, in CSV and JSON.

How did we count?

The source is the Bundesagentur für Arbeit job search (the Jobsuche API). We read every result page for eight stacks: React, Angular, Node.js, Java, Python, .NET, PHP and Flutter.

Three filters apply:

  • Temporary work and private placement are switched off in the search, so only regular jobs remain.
  • The title has to name a software role.
  • Postings by staffing and recruiting firms (61 in November) and by public bodies and large groups (202) are left out. This note is about small and mid-sized employers, the kind that buys from a studio.

"Listed for N days" counts from the first publication date. If an employer reposts a role under a new reference, the clock restarts. So every age here is a floor: the real wait is at least this long, often longer.

A posting that matches two stack searches counts in both stack rows and once in the totals.

Those 167 employers matter most to a seller. A company with one long-open role may just be picky. A company with two or more has a pattern: it needs more engineering capacity than it can hire.

Two snapshots are not a trend. What we can say: the number of postings fell by 114, the share listed 90 days or more rose by 2.3 points, and the median rose by six days. The count of employers with two or more long-open roles barely moved (169 to 167).

A median can rise simply because old postings stay up while fewer new ones arrive, which fits the fall in total postings. It does not by itself show that hiring got harder. The December snapshot will show whether the pattern holds.

By stack, the median listing time went up in the same period for React (66 to 87.5), Angular (87 to 111.5), Java (65.5 to 87.5) and PHP (106 to 119). These are single-month moves, and for the smaller stacks a few postings can shift them.

Why is a role open 90+ days a buying signal?

If a company has kept a developer role public for three months or more, two things are probably true. It has budget, because the role was approved and is still advertised. And it has an unmet need, because nobody has filled it.

For a studio or outsourcing firm, that is the situation where your offer is a real alternative: engineering capacity now, while the hire is pending or has failed. The ad also gives you the stack, the city and often the team, so you know what to say before you write.

The 167 employers with two or more long-open roles are the strongest version. They are short of people in more than one place.

This is a signal that the need exists, not that the company will buy. Many will hire in the end, and many prefer to. Some replies will be "we are still hoping to hire."

How do you use it without being creepy?

The posting is public, and the company published it to be read. Outreach feels invasive when it pretends to have found them by magic, or when it runs long on their hiring problems.

Two rules work:

  1. Name the role. "I saw you have a Senior Java developer open in Stuttgart" is honest and specific. It shows why you wrote.
  2. Ask one question. For example: "Would extra engineering capacity for this, while you keep hiring, help?" One question, no deck, no attached case study.

Do not write that they "struggle to hire." Do not guess why the role is open. Do not mention how many days it has been up. Say what you saw and ask.

Check the channel before you write. In Germany, cold email to a business generally needs prior consent under the unfair competition act (UWG, section 7), so we write to German companies on LinkedIn, not by email. This is how we work, not legal advice; the rules we follow by country are in our FAQ.

What are the limits of this data?

  • Reposting resets the date. A role reposted every 30 days looks new, so real waits are longer than shown.
  • A posting can be stale. Some employers forget to close an ad after the role is filled. The 180+ day group is the most likely to include these.
  • The API covers what employers list on the Bundesagentur platform. Roles advertised only on other boards, or filled through referrals, are not in it.
  • A role whose title does not name a software job is not counted.
  • Large groups, public bodies and staffing firms are dropped. If you sell to those, this dataset is not your market view.
  • One snapshot a month is coarse. It cannot tell you when a role was approved or whether the employer has hired someone since.

Employer names are not in the public dataset. They are in the lists we build for clients.
